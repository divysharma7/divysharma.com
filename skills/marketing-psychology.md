# Marketing Psychology & Mental Models

Use this skill when you need to apply behavioral science to marketing decisions.
Not theory for theory's sake -- every model here maps to a concrete move you can make
in copy, pricing, positioning, or product growth.

## 1. Foundational Mental Models

These are thinking tools. They don't describe buyer behavior directly -- they sharpen
how you frame problems before you pick tactics.

### First Principles Thinking

Strip a marketing problem down to its fundamental truths and rebuild from there. Most
marketers copy competitors. First-principles marketers ask "what does the buyer actually
need to believe before they buy?" and build the funnel backward from that.

**Marketing application:** Instead of copying a competitor's 14-day free trial because
"that's what SaaS companies do," ask: what is the minimum experience a user needs to
trust this product? Slack figured out that 2,000 messages sent was the activation
threshold -- not a time-based trial. They built onboarding around reaching that number,
not around a clock.

### Inversion

Instead of asking "how do I get more customers?", ask "what would guarantee I lose
every customer I have?" Then stop doing those things. Inversion surfaces hidden risks
that forward-only thinking misses.

**Marketing application:** Before launching a referral program, list every reason a
customer would refuse to refer you. "I'd be embarrassed if the product broke for my
friend" is more actionable than "we need better incentives." Fix the embarrassment
risk first, then add incentives.

### Second-Order Thinking

Every marketing action has immediate effects (first order) and downstream consequences
(second order). Most teams optimize for first-order only and get blindsided.

**Marketing application:** Offering a 60% discount drives sign-ups (first order). But
it also anchors your price perception at the discounted rate, attracts price-sensitive
customers who churn faster, and trains the market to wait for sales (second order).
Groupon's entire business model demonstrated what happens when you optimize only for
first-order effects.

### Circle of Competence

Know what you actually understand well enough to have an edge. Market within your
circle. Expand it deliberately, but don't pretend expertise you lack.

**Marketing application:** A bootstrapped dev-tools company has deep competence in
developer pain points. Their marketing should be technical blog posts and conference
talks, not glossy brand campaigns. The brand campaign is outside their circle -- they'll
produce something generic. The technical content will be genuinely useful and
differentiated because they know what they're talking about.

## 2. Buyer Psychology

These are cognitive biases and effects that shape how people evaluate, choose, and
justify purchases.

### Loss Aversion

People feel losses roughly 2x as intensely as equivalent gains. Losing $100 hurts more
than finding $100 feels good. This is the single most reliable bias in behavioral
economics.

**Marketing application:** Frame your value proposition around what the buyer loses
without you, not what they gain with you. "You're losing $4,200/month to manual data
entry" hits harder than "Save $4,200/month with automation." Trial expiration emails
should say "Your workspace and 47 saved reports will be deleted in 3 days" instead of
"Upgrade to keep your workspace."

### Anchoring

The first number a person sees distorts their judgment of all subsequent numbers. This
isn't subtle -- it works even when the anchor is obviously irrelevant (Tversky and
Kahneman showed this with a roulette wheel and UN population estimates).

**Marketing application:** Show the enterprise plan price ($299/mo) first on your
pricing page, even if most people buy the $49/mo plan. The $299 makes $49 feel cheap.
Consulting proposals should open with the annual cost of the problem ("This inefficiency
costs your team ~$180K/year") before presenting the $30K engagement fee.

### Social Proof

When uncertain, people look at what others are doing. This is not just "add
testimonials." The proof must be relevant to the buyer's identity and situation.

**Marketing application:** "12,000 companies use our product" is weak social proof.
"430 Series-A fintech startups use our compliance API" is strong -- it mirrors the
buyer's identity. Basecamp's "last week, 1,247 companies signed up" works because it
signals momentum, not just quantity. Display social proof at the exact moment of
decision (checkout page, pricing toggle, sign-up form), not buried on a testimonials
page nobody visits.

### IKEA Effect

People overvalue things they helped create. Even minimal participation in building
something increases perceived ownership and willingness to pay.

**Marketing application:** Let users customize their onboarding. Notion asks you to
name your workspace, pick templates, and set up a page structure before you even start
using it. By the time onboarding is "done," you feel like you built something -- and
abandoning it feels like loss (see: Loss Aversion). Product configurators on e-commerce
sites serve the same function: a "custom" sneaker you designed is harder to walk away
from than a pre-made one.

### Endowment Effect

Once people feel they own something, they value it more than they did before they owned
it. Related to loss aversion but distinct -- this is about the ownership itself creating
value.

**Marketing application:** Free trials work because of the endowment effect, not
because people are rationally evaluating ROI. After 14 days of using a tool, it feels
like theirs. The cancellation isn't "stopping a trial" -- it's "giving up my tool."
This is why reverse trials (start with full features, downgrade to free) outperform
traditional freemium. Zapier does this well: you get the premium tier for two weeks,
and going back to 5 zaps feels like a downgrade, not a return to baseline.

### Paradox of Choice

More options lead to decision paralysis, lower satisfaction, and higher regret. Barry
Schwartz demonstrated that people shown 24 jam varieties were 1/10th as likely to buy
as those shown 6.

**Marketing application:** Limit your pricing page to 3 tiers. If you must offer more,
use a recommendation badge ("Most Popular") to collapse the decision. Landing pages
should have one CTA, not three. When Procter & Gamble cut Head & Shoulders from 26
varieties to 15, sales went up 10%.

## 3. Persuasion: Cialdini's 6+1 Principles

Robert Cialdini's framework is the most field-tested persuasion model. The original
six were published in 1984; Unity was added in 2016.

### Reciprocity

People feel obligated to return favors. Give something genuinely useful before asking
for anything.

**Marketing application:** HubSpot built a billion-dollar business on reciprocity.
Free tools (Website Grader, CRM), free courses (HubSpot Academy), free templates --
all genuinely useful, no paywall. By the time a company needs marketing software,
HubSpot has deposited so much value that buying from them feels like the natural
next step, not a sales pitch.

### Scarcity

Limited availability increases perceived value. But fake scarcity destroys trust
permanently. Only use this when the constraint is real.

**Marketing application:** "3 spots left in this cohort" works for a live course
with an actual cap. "Only 2 left in stock!" on a digital product is insulting.
Superhuman's waitlist was real scarcity -- they manually onboarded users and
couldn't scale faster. The waitlist wasn't a growth hack; it was a genuine
constraint they turned into a signal. If your scarcity is manufactured, skip this
principle entirely.

### Authority

People defer to credible experts. Authority is demonstrated, not claimed.

**Marketing application:** "We're the leading platform" is a claim. Publishing
original research, getting cited by industry analysts, having your CEO quoted in
trade press -- that's demonstrated authority. Stripe's documentation is so
thorough and well-written that it became a moat: developers trust Stripe because
the docs signal deep competence. Your content quality is your authority signal.

### Commitment and Consistency

Once people take a small step, they're more likely to take the next one to stay
consistent with their self-image. This is the psychology behind the "foot in the
door" technique.

**Marketing application:** Get a micro-commitment before the big ask. A free
newsletter sign-up is a micro-commitment. A free account is a bigger one. Each
step makes the next feel consistent. Duolingo's daily streak exploits this
ruthlessly: missing a day breaks your streak, which breaks your self-image as
"someone who's learning Spanish." The commitment escalation is: install app ->
set a goal -> complete one lesson -> maintain streak -> subscribe to remove ads.

### Liking

People buy from people (and brands) they like. Liking comes from similarity,
compliments, cooperation, and familiarity.

**Marketing application:** Brand voice matters more than brand identity. Mailchimp's
playful voice makes a boring category (email marketing) feel human. They don't say
"enterprise-grade email marketing solution" -- they say "send better email." The
voice signals: we're like you, we don't take ourselves too seriously, we're on your
side. Founder-led content works for the same reason: a person is more likeable than
a logo.

### Unity

Cialdini's seventh principle (2016): shared identity creates influence. This is
deeper than liking -- it's "you're one of us."

**Marketing application:** Apple's "Think Different" campaign wasn't about product
specs. It was tribal: creative people belong here. Harley-Davidson sells identity,
not motorcycles. In B2B, this looks like vertical-specific positioning: "Built for
healthcare teams" creates unity that "Built for everyone" never will. Slack's
success in displacing email started within teams -- the shared workspace created an
in-group that non-Slack users were excluded from.

## 4. Pricing Psychology

Where behavioral economics meets revenue. These are not gimmicks -- they are
well-documented effects that shape how people perceive and evaluate prices.

### Charm Pricing

Prices ending in 9 outperform round numbers in most contexts. $39 feels categorically
cheaper than $40 because of left-digit anchoring (the brain encodes "3-something"
vs "4-something").

**Marketing application:** Use $X9 pricing for consumer products and lower-tier SaaS
plans where price sensitivity is high. Use round numbers ($50, $100, $500) for
premium and enterprise tiers -- round numbers signal quality and reduce the "deal
hunting" frame. A $99/mo plan says "affordable." A $500/mo plan says "serious."
Don't mix the signals.

### Decoy Effect (Asymmetric Dominance)

When choosing between two options, adding a third option that is clearly worse than
one but comparable to the other shifts preference toward the dominant option.

**Marketing application:** The Economist's famous pricing experiment:
- Digital only: $59
- Print only: $125
- Print + Digital: $125

Nobody picks print-only (it's the decoy), but its presence makes Print + Digital
look like a steal. Without the decoy, most people chose digital-only. With it,
84% chose the combo. If you have two plans and want to push people toward the
higher one, add a decoy that makes it look obviously superior.

### Price Anchoring

Different from general anchoring -- this is specifically about establishing a
reference price before revealing the actual price.

**Marketing application:** "Comparable tools charge $200/mo. Our Pro plan is
$79/mo." The $200 anchor makes $79 feel like a bargain even if the buyer would
have balked at $79 without context. "Was $149, now $99" uses temporal anchoring.
Show the crossed-out original price on sales pages, but only if the original
price was real and recent. Fake anchoring (inflating a "regular" price that
nobody ever paid) is illegal in many jurisdictions and always unethical.

### Bundle Framing

Bundling reduces the pain of paying by obscuring individual item costs. It also
shifts the evaluation from "is this item worth $X?" to "is this collection
worth $Y?" -- a much easier question to say yes to.

**Marketing application:** McDonald's Value Meal is the canonical example. Nobody
calculates whether the fries are fairly priced inside the bundle. In SaaS,
bundling works when features are complementary: "Analytics + Alerts + Reports
for $89/mo" is easier to justify than three separate $35/mo subscriptions
because the buyer evaluates the bundle holistically. Apple One bundles six
services at a price that makes each one feel nearly free.

## 5. Growth Models

These are structural models for how products and businesses scale. Not
psychology per se, but deeply connected to buyer behavior at scale.

### Flywheel

A self-reinforcing loop where each component accelerates the next. Unlike a
funnel (which leaks), a flywheel compounds.

**Marketing application:** Amazon's flywheel: lower prices -> more customers ->
more sellers -> better selection -> more customers -> lower costs -> lower prices.
Every investment in one part accelerates the whole system. For a content business:
great content -> organic traffic -> email subscribers -> social sharing -> more
traffic -> better data on what works -> better content. Map your flywheel. Find
the component with the most friction and reduce it. That's your highest-leverage
marketing investment.

### Network Effects

A product becomes more valuable as more people use it. Direct network effects
(phone network) vs. indirect (more Uber riders attract more drivers attract
more riders).

**Marketing application:** If your product has network effects, your marketing
strategy changes fundamentally. You need density, not breadth. Uber launched
city by city, not nationally, because 50 drivers in one city is useful; 50
drivers spread across 50 cities is useless. Facebook launched at Harvard, then
Ivy League, then colleges, then everyone. Your go-to-market should concentrate
on a beachhead market where you can achieve network density before expanding.

### Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD)

People don't buy products -- they hire them to do a job. The job is defined by
the situation, not the product category. Clayton Christensen's milkshake example:
morning commuters "hired" milkshakes for the job of "make my boring commute more
interesting and keep me full until lunch."

**Marketing application:** Stop segmenting by demographics. Segment by job. A
25-year-old founder and a 55-year-old VP might both "hire" your project
management tool for the job of "make sure nothing falls through the cracks when
my team is remote." Your copy should describe the job, not the user: "Never
miss a deadline when your team is in five time zones" instead of "Project
management for modern teams." Interview customers about the moment they decided
to look for a solution -- that moment reveals the job.

## Usage Notes

- Don't stack more than 2-3 principles in a single piece of copy. It gets manipulative fast.
- Every principle here can backfire if applied dishonestly. Fake scarcity, fake social proof, and fake authority erode trust permanently. The research on trust recovery is clear: it takes 8-12 positive interactions to recover from one broken promise.
- These models are descriptive, not prescriptive. They describe how people tend to behave, not how they should behave. Test everything in your specific context.
- When in doubt, apply the "would I feel tricked?" test. If a reasonable person would feel manipulated after understanding what you did, don't do it.
